# Little Play — Illustration Style Bible
## Single source of truth for illustration art direction, generation, approval, and handoff

**Purpose:** This document defines the visual language to follow when creating new standalone illustrations for Little Play. The images already present in `resources/General_reviews/Asset_Generation/assets_work_in_progress_bundle/` are the primary visual examples. This document translates their visible qualities into repeatable art-direction rules; it does not replace those images or grant production approval to any candidate asset.

**Status:** Art-direction specification. New artwork remains a candidate until it has been compared with the original references and explicitly approved.

---

## 1. The one-sentence art direction

Create **quietly charming, softly painted children's storybook illustrations**: rounded, simple, warm, tactile, gently imperfect, and immediately readable—like small pictures painted for a cozy picture book, not modern emoji, vector stickers, glossy 3D toys, or polished commercial mascots.

The intended emotional response is safe, calm, kind, familiar, and inviting. The picture should feel handmade without looking messy, and simple without looking generic.

## 2. Source of truth and reference hierarchy

Use the actual images in the bundle as the controlling visual reference, especially:

- **Bear:** the seated honey-brown bear is the character/material reference. Study its silhouette, body-to-head relationship, soft brown shading, cream muzzle, small facial features, subtle blush, and painted surface.
- **Star:** the plain golden-yellow five-point star is the shape/material reference. Study its plump rounded points, slightly organic contour, warm golden body, orange warmth toward the edges, and restrained paper-like texture. It has **no face, no outline, and no extra decoration**.

The bear and star are complementary, not interchangeable references: the bear guides friendly organic characters; the star guides simple non-character objects and warm color/shading. Use other files in the bundle as additional examples of the same family, but do not average away their defining qualities.

**Reference rules**
1. Inspect the original files at full size before creating each new asset.
2. Match the actual reference, not a verbal memory of it and not a generic “watercolor” preset.
3. Never redraw, replace, or silently “improve” the canonical bear or star.
4. If a reference is missing or cannot be inspected, record that limitation; do not substitute a vaguely similar image.
5. Treat files in this work-in-progress folder as style references/candidates, not automatic approval for runtime use.

## 3. Shape language and silhouette

### Required
- Favor soft, rounded, generous forms with no sharp or aggressive corners unless the real object requires them.
- Use gently uneven, organic contours. A little hand-drawn irregularity gives warmth; it should be subtle and intentional.
- Keep the main silhouette compact and easy to identify at a glance.
- Prefer a few large, clear masses over many small parts.
- Use friendly proportions: soft volumes, rounded ends, and stable, approachable poses.
- Keep the object visually balanced. It should feel planted and complete, not stretched, pinched, floating, or awkwardly cropped.
- Preserve the object's real-world identity. Stylization must not make a cow read as a dog, a pear as an apple, or a flower as an indistinct colored blob.

### Avoid
- Geometric perfection that feels like stock vector art.
- Needle-sharp points, spindly limbs, hard angular anatomy, or aggressive poses unless semantically necessary.
- Overly chibi proportions, enormous heads, tiny bodies, or exaggerated mascot anatomy that departs from the references.
- Complex silhouettes with many protrusions that collapse at small sizes.
- Excessive decorative bumps, curls, petals, spots, tufts, or accessories added only to make an image “cute.”

### Silhouette test
Squint at the image or view it as a small thumbnail. Its basic identity should remain recognizable from shape and broad color alone. Texture and tiny marks must never carry the recognition burden.

## 4. Paint, texture, and surface

The surface should suggest **soft watercolor washes with a delicate paper/colored-pencil character**. The look is tactile and illustrated, not a photographic simulation of paper.

### Required surface qualities
- Softly blended pigment within shapes, with gentle variation rather than a perfectly flat digital fill.
- Mild, natural color variation: slightly lighter and darker areas that feel painted into the object.
- Subtle grain or paper texture, visible on close inspection but quiet at normal display size.
- Soft transitions and edges. Contours remain clear, but should not look mechanically traced.
- A few controlled, painterly imperfections are welcome when they support the handmade feel.

### Keep the texture restrained
- Texture is a finishing quality, not a pattern. It should never compete with the silhouette, face, or teaching meaning.
- The object must still look clean and intentional; “hand-painted” does not mean dirty, distressed, blotchy, noisy, or unfinished.
- At small UI sizes, texture may become almost invisible. That is acceptable and preferable to excessive grain.

### Do not use
- Glossy plastic, metallic shine, wet-look specular highlights, glass effects, or toy-like 3D rendering.
- Photorealistic fur, feathers, pores, wood grain, skin, or detailed surface simulation.
- Strong digital gradient bands, airbrush halos, lens effects, bloom, or dramatic rim lighting.
- Heavy canvas texture, grunge, noise overlays, watercolor splashes outside the subject, or messy pigment blooms.
- Flat emoji/vector fills with perfectly uniform color and mathematically smooth edges.
- Thick black cartoon outlines, comic inking, or dark sticker borders.

## 5. Contours and edge treatment

- Let the painted boundary and value contrast define most edges; do not automatically draw a line around every object.
- Use soft, colored edge definition when needed to separate overlapping forms.
- Any contour should harmonize with the nearby fill—brown around a brown animal, muted warm/dark color where appropriate—not default black.
- Keep boundaries readable against the intended background without creating a cut-out sticker effect.
- Avoid a white halo, dark fringe, hard drop shadow, or opaque outline around the whole object.
- Small internal marks should be painted simply and consistently with the surface, not drawn as a different graphic style.

## 6. Palette and color relationships

The palette is **warm, natural, muted, and gentle**, with enough saturation to be cheerful for young children but not fluorescent or candy-neon.

### Palette families to favor
- **Warm neutrals:** honey brown, cocoa, chestnut, warm umber, beige, oat, ivory, cream.
- **Golden accents:** soft golden yellow, ochre, muted amber, a little warm orange near shaded edges.
- **Plant colors:** soft leaf green, muted sage, moss, gentle olive.
- **Cool accents:** dusty blue, softened sky blue, muted blue-green.
- **Warm accent colors:** peach, dusty rose, soft terracotta, restrained coral.
- **Light areas:** warm cream and softened off-white rather than stark, glowing white.

### Color handling
- Build each object from a small, coherent family of related colors. Usually one main hue, a few lighter/darker variations, and a small accent are enough.
- Shading should usually move toward a related warmer or deeper color, not toward pure black or a saturated complementary color.
- Highlights should be soft and broad, not tiny glossy white streaks.
- Keep neighboring colors harmonious. A bright accent can be used when it is inherent to the object, but it should not make the whole image feel louder than the bear and star.
- Preserve meaningful real-world color cues when they help recognition or learning—for example, an apple should not become an arbitrary pastel color just to fit the palette.

### Do not
- Use neon, fluorescent, electric, or aggressively saturated colors.
- Make every object equally vivid; a coherent family needs quiet colors and a restrained accent hierarchy.
- Add a thick white sticker border or a bright outer glow.
- Introduce unrelated colors solely for decoration.

**Important:** This is a relational palette, not a rigid set of exact hex values. Do not invent numerical color specifications and claim they were sampled from the references. If exact color matching is needed, sample the actual image files.

## 7. Light and shading

- Use soft, diffuse, broadly even light.
- Suggest volume with gentle transitions and a few well-placed darker areas.
- Keep shadows close to the form and low in contrast.
- Use warm edge shading where the reference calls for it, particularly on golden or warm-colored objects.
- Avoid dramatic directional lighting, deep cast shadows, sharp chiaroscuro, glowing highlights, or cinematic contrast.
- The object should look dimensional enough to feel painted, but never like a rendered 3D model.

If a ground/contact shadow is useful, keep it subtle and close to the feet/base. Do not create a large grey oval, opaque halo, or background-shaped shadow unless explicitly requested.

## 8. Faces and character expression

Faces are used only when appropriate to the object and consistent with the reference family.

### For friendly animals/characters
- Eyes should be small, simple, dark, and calm—never glossy anime eyes or large white cartoon eyeballs.
- Noses and mouths should use a few quiet marks with the minimum detail needed to communicate the expression.
- Expressions should feel gentle, relaxed, curious, or quietly cheerful.
- If cheeks are used, blush should be soft, small, and low-saturation, like the bear reference.
- Facial features must follow the form and feel painted into it, not pasted on as emoji graphics.
- Maintain enough spacing between features that the face reads clearly at the intended size.

### For objects
- Do **not** add a face by default. Food, stars, flowers, houses, tools, shapes, and scenery should remain ordinary illustrated objects unless a specific game requirement and explicit art direction calls for personification.
- In particular, the canonical star is plain and faceless. Do not add eyes, a smile, cheeks, sparkles, a hat, or other decoration.
- Do not turn every object into a mascot. Variety in the world comes from the objects themselves, not from putting a face on everything.

## 9. Detail budget and visual hierarchy

Each asset should have a deliberate detail budget.

1. **Primary read:** recognizable outer shape and main color.
2. **Secondary read:** a few defining parts (for example, a muzzle, leaf, stem, wing, ear, or wheel).
3. **Surface finish:** restrained painted shading and faint texture.
4. **Optional accent:** only when it clarifies the object or is part of the canonical reference.

Most assets should stop after these levels. Extra decoration needs a clear reason.

- Do not add intricate feather layering, individual hairs, many tiny spots, elaborate patterns, tiny accessories, or multiple competing highlights.
- Avoid detail that disappears at mobile size but makes the full-size image busy.
- Keep detail density similar across an asset family. One highly rendered animal beside a very simple animal will look like it came from another game.
- Prefer clear educational forms over natural-history accuracy. The picture must teach the intended word/object quickly.

## 10. Composition, pose, and framing

### Default composition
- One main subject per file, unless the inventory item explicitly requires a group or a scene.
- Subject centered or optically centered, with comfortable breathing room around all sides.
- Entire subject visible. Do not cut off ears, leaves, petals, feet, tails, stems, or other identifying parts.
- Use a calm, uncluttered composition with no unrelated props or background story elements.
- Keep the visual mass balanced within the canvas. Account for asymmetrical forms (such as a long tail, stem, or sail) when centering optically.
- Use a natural, stable pose and a readable view angle. Avoid extreme perspective or dramatic foreshortening.
- For related items, keep apparent scale and framing consistent, not necessarily identical bounding-box dimensions.

### Background
The art direction is defined by the **subject's paint, palette, shapes, and finish**, not by a mandatory background removal process.

- A plain white or warm off-white background is acceptable for work-in-progress images when that is the requested deliverable.
- A transparent background is preferred for eventual standalone runtime objects when required by implementation, but it is not a prerequisite for style review in the work-in-progress bundle.
- Do not add a room, landscape, gradient panel, decorative frame, ground, scene, or colored backdrop unless the inventory/use case explicitly needs it.
- Do not spend time removing a background when the current task only asks for the illustration and the background is acceptable.
- If transparency is used, edges must be clean with no white matte, colored fringe, or semi-transparent haze around the subject.

### Canvas and export
- The existing art-direction baseline recommends 512×512 PNG for a typical standalone object; use a larger canvas only when actual display/detail needs justify it.
- Keep the original proportions of the subject. Do not stretch a portrait subject into a square or squash a wide object.
- PNG is the expected image format for the asset workflow unless a specific use case calls for another format.
- Do not crop tightly to the point that soft contours or identifying parts touch the canvas edge.
- Do not upscale a low-resolution reference and present the enlarged file as newly created high-quality artwork.

## 11. Consistency across the asset family

A set should feel as though the same illustrator painted every picture for one book.

Keep consistent:
- softness of edges;
- amount and scale of visible texture;
- contrast and shadow strength;
- warmth and saturation;
- contour treatment;
- complexity and detail density;
- facial feature scale and expression restraint;
- subject scale and amount of empty space;
- degree of shape irregularity;
- light direction and overall softness.

Do not make every asset identical in color or silhouette. Consistency is the shared treatment, not sameness of subject.

### Family check
Place the candidate next to the bear, star, and the closest existing subject in the bundle. Ask:
- Does it appear to belong in the same illustrated world immediately?
- Is it equally soft and restrained, or does it look too crisp, glossy, busy, dark, saturated, or childish in a different way?
- Does it have the same level of finish?
- Does its shape remain recognizable at small size?
- Does its background/edge treatment fit the other assets?
If it looks like an emoji, vector icon, sticker, 3D render, stock illustration, or a different artist's style, revise it.

## 12. Specific guidance by asset category

### Animals
- Use soft, rounded body masses and simple, believable anatomy.
- Keep legs, ears, tails, horns, beaks, and wings thick/clear enough to survive downscaling.
- Include only the markings needed to identify the species.
- Use simple painted fur/feather suggestions rather than individual strands or layered micro-details.
- Avoid aggressive teeth, claws, exaggerated eyebrows, or highly theatrical expressions unless the teaching context specifically needs them.
- Do not use a generic same-body template that makes every species look alike; retain the distinguishing silhouette.

### Food and fruit
- Keep the whole item readable through shape and natural color.
- Use restrained soft highlights and shaded edges to suggest roundness, not a polished waxy or wet surface.
- Leaves, stems, seeds, and segments should be few, clear, and appropriately sized.
- Do not over-render skin pores, tiny seeds, or glossy reflections.
- Preserve color distinctions when the game teaches them (for example, green versus red apple).

### Plants, flowers, and nature
- Use gentle organic curves and simple leaf/petal groups.
- Avoid dense botanical detail, intricate veins, excessive petals, or ornamental flourishes.
- Keep plant parts distinct enough for sequence/recognition activities.
- Natural colors should remain soft but identifiable.

### Simple objects, toys, tools, and household items
- Simplify geometry without losing the object's defining construction.
- Use the same softly painted material treatment as the animals and star.
- Keep handles, openings, blades, wheels, strings, or other important parts clearly separated.
- Do not add faces or extra decoration unless explicitly required.

### Shapes and interface controls
- Basic geometric teaching shapes and standard controls are not automatically watercolor illustration tasks.
- Prefer crisp, accessible SVG/CSS geometry for circles, squares, triangles, arrows, sound controls, restart, close, and other standard interface icons.
- Do not force every UI symbol into a painted PNG. The overall experience should be coherent, while interface controls remain clear and functional.

## 13. Generation brief template

Use this structure for every new image prompt. Replace bracketed text with the exact asset details and adapt the category-specific anatomy as needed.

> Create one standalone illustration of **[exact subject]** for a cozy early-childhood storybook game. Match the supplied Little Play bear and golden star references: soft watercolor wash with very subtle paper/colored-pencil grain; warm natural, gently muted colors; rounded, slightly organic shapes; soft painted shading; restrained contrast; quiet handmade charm; minimal, clean details; readable silhouette at small mobile size. Keep the subject anatomically/object-wise recognizable and visually balanced, with a calm friendly feeling. Use [appropriate expression / no face]. One subject only, fully visible, centered with comfortable breathing room. [Plain white or warm off-white background is acceptable / transparent background if specifically required]. No added props or scene unless requested. The result must look like it belongs beside the exact reference images.

### Always include the relevant exclusions
- No photorealism, 3D rendering, glossy plastic, vector emoji, stock-icon look, or anime styling.
- No thick black outline, white sticker border, glow, hard drop shadow, or dramatic lighting.
- No excessive gradient, tiny detail, heavy texture, decorative sparkles, or ornamental extras.
- No unrequested face or accessories.
- No text, labels, watermark, logo, collage, contact sheet, or multiple alternatives in one image.
- Do not alter or imitate the exact reference subject as a replacement; create the requested new subject in the same visual family.

A prompt is not a guarantee of a match. Inspect and revise the actual output against the reference images.

## 14. Review and approval checklist

Review every candidate at both full size and the approximate size at which it will appear in-game.

### Style
- [ ] Reads as cozy storybook painting, not emoji/vector/3D/stock art.
- [ ] Uses soft, natural, harmonious colors.
- [ ] Texture is subtle and does not obscure the form.
- [ ] Edges are soft but the silhouette is clear.
- [ ] Shading is gentle and integrated with the painted surface.
- [ ] Detail density matches the existing bundle.
- [ ] No unrequested face, props, background scene, text, border, or effects.

### Subject and composition
- [ ] Correct subject and unmistakable defining features.
- [ ] Whole subject visible; no accidental cropping.
- [ ] Balanced framing and comfortable breathing room.
- [ ] Readable when reduced to a small mobile UI size.
- [ ] No distortions, stray marks, unwanted objects, or confusing overlaps.
- [ ] Related assets have comparable apparent scale and finish.

### Technical handoff
- [ ] Filename matches the agreed stable asset ID.
- [ ] Dimensions and format are recorded.
- [ ] Background choice matches the current task and eventual use.
- [ ] No visible background fringe, compression artifacts, or damaged edges.
- [ ] Intended game/component and reuse plan are documented.
- [ ] Candidate is marked WIP until explicit approval.
- [ ] Only explicitly approved assets may be copied into runtime asset directories.

**Reject or revise** any image that fails a core style test, even if the subject is cute or technically well drawn. Subject attractiveness alone is not evidence of style consistency.

## 15. Workflow and approval boundaries

1. Confirm the exact asset ID, semantic use, and inventory status before generating.
2. Inspect the actual bear/star references and the closest category example.
3. Generate one subject at a time or a very small, reviewable batch.
4. Compare the output side-by-side with the references at full size and target size.
5. Record what matches, what differs, and whether another iteration is needed.
6. Keep all candidates in the work-in-progress area until they are explicitly approved.
7. Update the progress ledger accurately; never mark an image approved merely because it was generated, uploaded, or included in the bundle.
8. Only after approval, prepare the agreed runtime export and implementation mapping.

This document defines style, not inventory approval. The existing source inventory remains subject to its own semantic review and user approval. Do not interpret this document as approval to generate every listed emoji or replace any excluded character sprite, existing good illustration, 3D model, or interface icon.

## 16. Final north star

**Soft, warm, simple, tactile, recognizable, and consistent.**

Every new illustration should feel like it was painted by the same gentle storybook illustrator who made the canonical bear and star. When deciding between adding detail and preserving clarity, choose clarity. When deciding between stronger effects and a quieter painted finish, choose the quieter finish. When uncertain, compare with the actual reference files rather than improvising a new style.

## 17. Production workflow, reuse, and implementation contract

This section consolidates the operational rules that previously lived in `ART_DIRECTION.md`. This file is now the single authoritative document for illustration style and the associated asset workflow.

### Canonical references and storage
- Use the exact user-supplied bear and star files as fixed references; do not substitute similar images.
- If the original reference files cannot be found in the working resources, request that they be added under `resources/General_reviews/Asset_Generation/references/`. Do not silently recreate them.
- Preserve prompts, reference notes, generation decisions, and asset mapping documentation under `resources/General_reviews/Asset_Generation/`.
- Keep generated candidates in the work-in-progress area until explicitly approved.
- Put only explicitly approved runtime assets in the appropriate `game/assets/images/` subfolder.

### Canvas, export, and offline operation
- Default standalone-object canvas: **512×512 PNG**. Use 1024×1024 only when the intended display size or detail requirements justify it.
- Production standalone objects should use optimized PNG with alpha transparency when supported and required by the runtime use case.
- Keep the subject's proportions, leave comfortable padding, and ensure identifying parts are not clipped.
- A white or warm off-white background is acceptable for WIP/style review when requested. Transparency is a runtime/implementation requirement where needed, not a reason to delay an illustration-only task.
- Keep the application offline-first: do not add remote runtime image URLs or external runtime dependencies for artwork.

### Canonical assets and reuse
- Create one canonical asset per distinct concept, not a separate image for every game.
- Reuse the same approved apple, star, flower, animal, or other object across games when its meaning, framing, and scale remain suitable.
- Do not create arbitrary alternate poses, color variants, or duplicates unless a real gameplay or teaching distinction requires them.
- Preserve suitable existing illustrations; this is a targeted replacement of selected emoji visuals, not a general repaint of the application.
- Standard UI controls—back, arrows, sound, restart, play/pause, close, hints, and similar controls—should use a coherent SVG/CSS icon family or text where appropriate, not watercolor pictures by default.
- Never create illustrations that replace the existing Explorer or Kitty character sprites/animation frames.
- In 3D Racing, only eligible emoji glyphs/UI decorations may be considered. Preserve real 3D models, meshes, geometry, scenery, lighting, and rendering.

### Approval workflow
1. Verify the asset's ID, meaning, actual source/render location, and inventory status before generation.
2. Generate one asset at a time or a small, reviewable batch; do not generate speculative inventory rows as if already approved.
3. Compare every candidate with the exact bear and star references and the closest related image in the WIP bundle.
4. Inspect at full size and at actual or representative in-game display sizes; test against relevant backgrounds when available.
5. Record objective observations and remaining issues in the progress tracker.
6. Mark a candidate approved only after explicit user review. Generation, upload, inclusion in the bundle, or technical validity is not approval.
7. Save the final prompt/reference notes and file mapping for approved assets.
8. Only after approval, prepare the runtime export and implementation mapping.

### Required handoff record for every approved asset
- Stable asset ID and agreed filename.
- Image dimensions and file format; alpha/transparency status when relevant.
- Exact source glyph(s) or existing visual being replaced.
- Exact game/component and source location(s) where it is used.
- Intended runtime destination under `game/assets/images/`.
- Approved prompt and reference notes.
- Reuse plan across games, including any scale/framing caveats.
- Cropping, scaling, animation, or background notes where relevant.
- QA status and outstanding caveats.

### Implementation AI contract
The implementation phase must map approved asset IDs only to the agreed render sites. It must preserve layout, gameplay, accessibility, and behavior; avoid inventing or altering artwork; avoid replacing excluded sprites, existing good illustrations, or 3D content; update offline/cache manifests where required; and test each affected game. Any uncertainty about a mapping or the semantic role of an emoji must be resolved against the source inventory rather than guessed.

### Relationship to the inventory and progress tracker
This style bible defines the single source of truth for **visual style and asset workflow**. It does not replace the separate semantic inventory of candidate emoji/render sites or the progress ledger:
- `ASSET_INVENTORY.md` records candidate assets, source use sites, semantic treatment, and review status.
- `PROGRESS.md` records audit, approval, generation, QA, and implementation progress.
Those documents must refer to this style bible for visual requirements and must not maintain a competing copy of the style rules.

### Explicit scope gate
Do not interpret this style document as approval to generate every candidate in the inventory. The inventory remains subject to semantic review and explicit user approval. Do not change game mechanics, replace existing character sprites, regenerate good existing illustrations without a reason, or convert real 3D Racing objects into 2D images.


## 18. Style-locking loop: every approved image strengthens the standard

The style bible is a **living, versioned standard**. Each explicitly approved image is a new piece of evidence about what Little Play's visual style means in practice. Approval must therefore do two jobs: approve that asset for its stated use, and capture any reusable visual lesson that the image demonstrates. The goal is progressive convergence: each batch should make future results more consistent and reduce ambiguity.

### 18.1 What counts as evidence
- **Primary evidence:** the canonical bear and star references, plus any other explicitly designated fixed reference.
- **Approved exemplar:** a new image the user has explicitly approved for the intended use. It can demonstrate how the style applies to a category such as animals, food, plants, or objects.
- **Candidate/WIP:** an image awaiting review. It may be useful for comparison, but it must not silently change the standard.
- **Rejected or superseded candidate:** useful as a negative example only when the reason for rejection is recorded; it must not be used as a positive style reference.
- A generated, uploaded, technically valid, or previously unreviewed image is **not** an approved exemplar.

### 18.2 Required action after each explicit approval
For every image the user explicitly approves:

1. **Record the approval accurately** in the progress ledger, including the exact filename/asset ID, approval date or commit context where available, and approved scope. Do not infer approval from silence or from a request to continue.
2. **Compare it against the fixed references and existing approved exemplars.** Identify which visual qualities it confirms and whether it introduces a genuinely useful category-specific lesson.
3. **Update this style bible only with reusable findings.** Examples include a proven range of animal-head/body proportions, a successful level of feather simplification, how much grain survives at small size, a repeatable leaf shape, or a validated balance of warm highlights and edge shading.
4. **Make the rule operational.** State what to repeat, where it applies, and what to avoid. Prefer observable guidance over subjective praise such as “cute” or “nice.”
5. **Add the approved image to the exemplar index** below, with its category and the specific style lesson it establishes. Link to the exact repository file when possible.
6. **Check for conflicts** with existing rules. If a new exemplar seems to contradict the standard, do not silently add both rules. Determine whether the difference is category-specific, a one-off approved exception, or a real art-direction change. Document the resolution.
7. **Record the documentation change** in the same commit or a clearly linked follow-up commit where practical, so the image approval and its learned rule remain traceable.
8. **Use the updated guidance on the next generation.** The next prompt/review must consider the relevant approved exemplars, not just repeat the original generic prompt.

### 18.3 Keep the standard stable, not endlessly subjective
- An approved image does not automatically rewrite the style. Extract only properties that are intentional, visible, reusable, and consistent with the fixed references.
- Do not promote accidental artifacts, generation defects, background quirks, inconsistent linework, or technical compromises into style rules.
- Do not generalize an animal-specific feature to fruit or objects unless the evidence supports that transfer.
- If the user approves an image despite a style deviation, record whether approval is **asset-specific exception** or an intentional **style-standard change**. Ask for clarification when that distinction materially affects future work.
- A single exemplar can establish a useful category rule, but repeated successful examples increase confidence. Mark tentative observations as provisional rather than writing them as mandatory rules.
- Keep the bear and star as the fixed anchor references unless the user explicitly changes that decision. New exemplars refine application of the style; they do not replace the anchors by default.
- Preserve previous decisions and explain material changes. Do not silently rewrite history or claim a rule was always part of the standard.

### 18.5 Per-asset learning record

For each approved image, record a compact note in the progress tracker or linked review note:

- **What matched:** observable features that fit the style.
- **What was learned:** reusable rule or category-specific refinement.
- **What should not be copied:** any one-off feature, artifact, or exception.
- **Scale check:** what remains legible at actual in-game size.
- **Standard impact:** no change / clarification / category-specific rule / approved exception / explicit art-direction change.
- **Next-use instruction:** how the learning should affect the next prompt or review.

The style bible should remain the consolidated source of truth; do not create multiple competing style guides. Detailed per-image evidence may live in the progress ledger or review notes, but any generally applicable style rule must be consolidated here.

### 18.6 The convergence test

Before starting each new batch, compare the planned prompt and prior approved exemplars against this checklist:

- [ ] The fixed bear and star anchors are still respected.
- [ ] Relevant approved category exemplars have been reviewed.
- [ ] New learning from the previous approval has been applied.
- [ ] No rejected candidate is being treated as a positive exemplar.
- [ ] Any exception or conflict has been resolved and documented.
- [ ] The prompt describes the actual subject and its essential identifying features, not just the style.
- [ ] The review will judge both standalone quality and family consistency.

After the batch, ask whether the result confirms the standard or exposes a genuine gap. If it exposes a gap, update the standard only after the user has approved the image or the underlying direction. The goal is not to make the document longer after every image; the goal is to make it more precise, evidence-based, and predictive.
